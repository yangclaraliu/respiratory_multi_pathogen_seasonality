Influenza viruses and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) are respiratory pathogens that impose a large global disease burden, causing an estimated hundreds of millions of infections annually and contributing to approximately 300{,}000--600{,}000 respiratory deaths worldwide each year.

In many temperate and some subtropical regions, the disease burden associated with these pathogens exhibits strong within-year cyclical patterns, often peaking in winter or cooler months.
Such within-year patterns may be driven by environmental and climatic factors (e.g. temperature and humidity), human behaviours (e.g. school terms, holiday travel, and indoor crowding), and immune dynamics (e.g. waning and boosting of population immunity).
These within-year patterns vary by region and pathogen.
Between-year cyclical patterns may also occur, driven by changes in circulating pathogens (e.g., antigenic drift, antagonistic or synergistic interactions between pathogens), demographics (e.g., population turnover), environmental and climatic patterns (e.g., El Niño/La Niña), immune dynamics (e.g., waning immunity), and behaviours (e.g., more risk-averse behaviours following large outbreaks).

% concurrent outbreaks + public health implications
Within- and between-year temporal dynamics sometimes cause the circulation of these seasonal respiratory pathogens to peak concurrently.
Such concurrent outbreaks of seasonal respiratory pathogens have been observed in many settings, such as in Europe, the United States, and China, most of which involve both the influenza virus and RSV.
These concurrent outbreaks pose substantial challenges to public health systems by increasing demand for healthcare resources and potentially overwhelming healthcare systems.
Therefore, to better support health systems' preparation for "winter pressures", it is crucial to understand the risk of concurrent respiratory virus outbreaks.

By far, most relevant research around the timing of seasonal respiratory pathogens has focused on deriving and understanding their within-year patterns.
The between-year patterns, however, have rarely been considered due to data constraints.
Such analyses require long-term surveillance data at fine spatial and temporal resolution to be simultaneously available for multiple pathogens.
Coarse spatial resolution, such as administrative level one (e.g. provincial or state-level) aggregate data, often masks spatial variations in both within- and between-year patterns, and thus dampens out the potential signal for concurrent outbreaks.
Furthermore, validating the role played by between-year patterns in a robust manner requires multiple study populations, which in turn requires the type of data mentioned above—already rare—to be available for multiple locations.

Statistical models are useful tools for understanding the role of within- and between-year variations in seasonal respiratory pathogens and for anticipating near-term trends in terms of when the greatest burden of these pathogens is expected to occur.
Seasonal and near-term forecasting is well established for respiratory infections such as influenza, with common targets including season onset, peak timing, and peak intensity.
However, most existing predictive models focus on single-pathogen dynamics and are designed to forecast the health outcomes attributable to individual pathogens (e.g. the number of RSV hospitalisations expected in coming weeks).
Here, we provide a complementary framework to provide seasonal-level forecasts for the probability of a concurrent outbreak and the possible temporal interval until the next concurrent outbreak.
This addresses a different aspect of respiratory public health risks that has been understudied despite being important to health predicting the probability and timing of concurrent outbreaks remains comparatively underexplored.
%Seasonal level forecasts have been widely used in the context of vector-borne infectious diseases, and therefore their value to managing respiratory health warrants further exploration.
To develop the seasonal forecasting framework for concurrent outbreaks, we face two key technical challenges.
First, methods designed for identifying within-year cyclical components may mischaracterise between-year patterns as residual noises. 
Second, for most locations, concurrent outbreaks are rare events.
With finite observational windows, it is challenging to distinguish between events that "will not occur" and events that "have not yet occurred".

To address known technical challenges, we compiled city-level seasonal influenza and RSV surveillance time series covering more than five years from across mainland China.

Using these data, we developed an analytical framework that combines time-series analysis and survival analysis to quantify the probability of a concurrent outbreak and the interval to the subsequent concurrent outbreak. 
Although the estimates we provide focus on seasonal influenza and RSV, our framework is widely adaptable to studying other respiratory pathogens with continuous measured data describing their disease burden.
