\subsection{Model comparison}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../figures/fig2_v3.png}
\caption{The association between within-year cycle lengths and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values by pathogen and city. Each purple point represents a model with only within-year cycles. Each Tukey boxplot represents the distribution of AIC values for models with the corresponding within-year cycle length and the full range of between-year cycle lengths tested.}
\label{fig:fig2}
\end{figure}

We compared the performance of the models only adjusted for within-year cycles against the models adjusted for both within- and between-year cycles using AIC values (\hyperref[fig:fig2]{Figure 2}). 
AIC values combine model fit (log-likelihood) with complexity penalties, and therefore the scales depend on both data characteristics and model structure.
Therefore, AIC value comparisons are only meaningful if the underlying data set remains the same (i.e. the same city and pathogen combination).
Lower AIC values indicate better model performance.
In nearly all combinations of pathogen, city, and within-year cycle length, some models adjusted for both within- and between-year cycles performed better than the models only adjusted for within-year cycles after penalising for increased degrees of freedom.
The only exceptions were RSV in Wuhan - models with only annual cycles performed better than all other models tested.
Thus, for seasonal influenza and RSV, significant between-year cycles may improve model performance beyond annual cycles alone.

For RSV, the relationship between within-year cycle length and AIC was often U-shaped, with the minimum at ~12 months, indicating a best-fitting annual cycle across many city–pathogen combinations. 
In contrast, seasonal influenza showed a less consistent pattern: the AIC minimum frequently deviated from 12 months, implying that the optimal seasonal period is not strictly annual. 
This could reflect time-varying or heterogeneous periodicity (e.g., episodes of slightly shorter or longer cycles that average to $\approx$ 12 months over the long term) or unaccounted-for variation (e.g., due to factors not included in the model), despite long-term epidemiology appearing annual.

In several panels, the AIC values near the minimum were very densely distributed (i.e. small dispersion), indicating that several model specifications (from up to 90 MSTL model specifications) produce comparably performing models.
Accordingly, in forward simulations and concurrent outbreak analysis, we carried forward both the optimal models and their comparable models ($\Delta$AIC $< 7$).
The number of comparable models for influenza ranged from 2 (in Xi'an, Lanzhou, Wenzhou, and Yunfu) to 20 (in Wuhan and Beijing); that for RSV ranged from 2 (in Wenzhou and Yunfu) to 39 in Beijing.
The full list detailing the number of comparable models by city and pathogen is available in the Supplementary Material (Table S1).

The optimal within-year cycle lengths and between-year cycle lengths for each city and pathogen combination are presented in \hyperref[fig:fig3]{Figure 3} (A) and (B).
These results reflect only the optimal models (the single best-performing model with lowest AIC per city-pathogen combination); their comparable models (those with ΔAIC < 7) are not included.
Similar to the results presented in \hyperref[fig:fig2]{Figure 2}, most within-year cycles were approximately 12 months for RSV and were not strictly annual for seasonal influenza.
The between-year cycles selected for the optimal models were much more variable.
The estimated lengths of within- and between-year cycles are not strongly associated with the length of the time series (\hyperref[fig:fig3]{Figure 3}(C)) or their temporal resolution (\hyperref[fig:fig3]{Figure 3}(D)).
Looking across all optimal and their comparable models, the between-year cycles for influenza had a mean of 28 months in the North and 27 months in the South; those for RSV had a median of 25 months in the North and 28 months in the South (\hyperref[fig:fig3]{Figure 3}(E)). 
These differences are not statistically significant by region for either pathogen, which may be explained by small sample size and thus limited statistical power.
The between-year cycles were highly variable (i.e. IQR > 6 months) for Suzhou (influenza), Wuhan (RSV) and Guangzhou (RSV) (see Supplementary Material Figure S1). 

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../figures/figure5.png}
\caption{Proportion of years with concurrent outbreaks of seasonal influenza and RSV under different risk thresholds by city. Distributions are based on 1000 forward simulation trajectories using both the optimal and comparable models. Risk thresholds are defined by joint exceedance of the 70th, 80th, and 90th percentiles (corresponding to the top 30\%, 20\%, and 10\%) of each city's observed disease burden history for both pathogens. Each ridge plot shows the density of the per-trajectory proportion of projected years with concurrent outbreaks (one proportion per simulation trajectory), calculated over the available projection horizon for each city--pathogen time series.}
\label{fig:fig4}
\end{figure}

Forward projection not only reflects the within- and between-year cycles we estimated, but also the underlying city-specific epidemic histories. 
Using both optimal and comparable models, we demonstrate that there are non-negligible risks of concurrent outbreaks of influenza and RSV in the cities we investigated (\hyperref[fig:fig4]{Figure 4}).
Using the strictest risk threshold (joint top 10 percentile, i.e. simultaneously observing influenza and RSV exceeding the 90th percentile of the city's respective observed history)), Wenzhou was projected to experience the most frequent concurrent outbreaks (i.e. 25\% of years projected), followed by Xi'an and Wuhan (15\% of years projected).
Beijing and Lanzhou were projected to not experience any concurrent outbreaks given this strict risk threshold.
Under less strict thresholds, we found that the ranking by frequency remains consistent.
When concurrent outbreak was defined by joint top 20 percentile (i.e. simultaneously observing influenza and RSV exceeding the 80th percentile of the city's respective observed history), Beijing and Lanzhou were expected to observe some concurrent outbreaks (i.e. 10\% and 5.9\% of years projected, respectively).
A complete table of median values of distributions presented in \hyperref[fig:fig4]{Figure 4} can be found in the Supplementary Material Table S2.

\label{fig:fig6}

Using the observed intervals between consecutive concurrent outbreak years from the forward simulations, we estimated the distribution of temporal gaps to the next potential concurrent outbreak for each city and threshold combination (\hyperref[fig:fig6]{Figure 6}) using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. 
Under the strictest risk threshold (joint top 10 percentile), the median temporal gap to the next concurrent outbreak was the shortest in Wenzhou, Xi'an, and Wuhan (two, three, and three years respectively).
In other words, in these cities, at least 50\% of the projection paths experienced another concurrent outbreak within two or three years after the last concurrent outbreak.
In Suzhou and Yunfu, we expected longer temporal gaps between concurrent outbreaks (six and seven years, respectively).
In Beijing, Lanzhou, and Guangzhou, concurrent outbreaks as defined by the strictest threshold were not likely based on the projection paths.
Relaxing the joint epidemic threshold to the top 20 percentile (i.e. simultaneously observing influenza and RSV exceeding the 80th percentile of the city's respective observed history), the median temporal gap ranged from one to six years.
Beijing and Guangzhou, which previously were not expecting any concurrent outbreaks, were now projected to experience them next in five or six years, respectively.
Lanzhou was still projected to not experience any concurrent outbreaks given this outbreak risk threshold.
A full table of the results can be found in the Supplementary Material Table S3.
